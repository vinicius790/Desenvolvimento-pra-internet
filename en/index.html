All you need to know about Google Glass

by Pedro Henrique

Updated on December 11, 2017

Editor’s note (2026): this story describes Glass as it was in 2013, still a prototype, and is kept as a course exercise. What happened next: the Explorer Edition was sold to developers from 2013 for US$1,500, reached the general public in the United States in 2014 and had its sales ended in January 2015. Google later released business versions (Enterprise Edition, in 2017 and 2019) and discontinued the product in 2023.

What it is

Google Glass is an accessory shaped like a pair of glasses that lets users interact with all kinds of content in augmented reality. Also known as Project Glass (opens in a new tab), the device can take pictures from voice commands, send instant messages and make video calls. When it was announced, its release was expected for 2014 at an estimated price of US$1,500. In 2013 Google Glass was still being tested and already had a video shot entirely with the device. The search company had also filed anti-theft and screen unlock patents for the accessory.

Release date

In 2013 there was still no specific, official release date for the device. Demos were expected from that year on, with the product reaching stores in 2014 at the earliest, which turned out to be right: the Explorer Edition was opened to the general public in the United States in May 2014.

How it works

According to sources close to Google, the glasses have a small LCD or AMOLED display at the top, in front of the user’s eyes. With a camera and GPS, you can find your way around and select options by moving your head.

What you can do with Google Glass

Google’s promotional video shows that you can become a kind of “superhuman”: the device can tell how many meters you are from your destination, whether the subway is open or closed, show the weather and your calendar, and even let you set up meetings with voice commands alone.

What’s new in Glass

Google has finally revealed the full Google Glass specs, and with them a surprise not yet seen on the market: the search giant will use an audio system based on bone conduction. Through the temples of the glasses, sound will reach the user’s ear by means of tiny vibrations in certain bones of the head, with no speaker of any kind.

Besides the audio surprise, the display mounted in front of the user’s eye also drew attention. It will have a resolution of 640 x 360 pixels which, in proportion, would match a 25-inch high definition monitor placed 2.5 meters away from the viewer.
